Development Zone Policies, Industrial Agglomeration, and Household Income: Evidence from China R&R · JRS

When local governments attract firms into development zones with tax preferences rather than genuine market agglomeration, do households end up paying for the expansion of industry? This paper says yes.

Data 2000–2017 county panel · 2,598 provincial+ zones · 4,455 county-years Design Staggered DID → CSDID · PSM · event study · Oster δ Status Major revision, JRS

Abstract — China's development zones, established to expand industrial capacity, have been a central policy tool of local governments, but their impact on household income remains poorly understood. We construct a three-sector conceptual framework of households, government, and enterprises to examine how development zones affect the growth of per capita disposable income. Using county-level panel data from 2000 to 2017, we find that development zones significantly reduce per capita disposable income by approximately 1.68%. Zones attract firms primarily through policy-induced tax-preference effects rather than market agglomeration. The expansion of above-scale enterprise output partially mediates the negative effects on households. The adverse effect is larger in fiscally pressured counties. These findings highlight the distributional consequences of place-based industrial policies.

Population Agglomeration and the Missing Consumption Dividend: Exclusionary Urbanization and Hukou Segmentation in Chinese Cities ↗ R&R · HSSC

Density is a production engine but not automatically a consumption engine: cities have integrated migrants into production while leaving them outside the arrangements that turn urban location into consumption.

Data 226 cities south of the Hu Line, 2003–2022 (3,131 obs) + CHFS 2017/2019 (19,417 households) Design TWFE · IV (1820 historical density) · quadratic · 4 hukou-group regressions Status Major revision, HSSC · Preprint ↗

Abstract — Population agglomeration is widely regarded as an engine of growth, yet in China it has coexisted with a persistently low household consumption share. This paper documents a missing consumption dividend of density and traces it to exclusionary urbanization: cities have integrated migrants into production while leaving them partially outside the arrangements that convert urban income and location into consumption. Using a panel of 226 Chinese cities over 2003–2022, the net effect of population density on per-capita consumption follows an inverted U with a turning point near 256 people per km²; roughly three-quarters of city-years lie on the downward-sloping segment, and instrumenting density with historical (1820) density amplifies the negative effect. Household evidence from the CHFS shows the non-housing consumption response to city tier is positive for local-hukou households, weaker for non-local non-agricultural households, and absent for non-local agricultural households — who bear housing costs that rise for everyone while being denied the wage premium that agglomeration generates.

The Impact of Generative AI Exposure on Skill Diversity and Wage Inequality: Evidence from China's City–Occupation Labor Market Working Paper

Is generative AI an equalizer or a new maker of inequality? Through the lens of skill diversity, the answer is both: convergence within occupations, divergence between occupations, and selective equalization of wages.

Data 170 cities × 99 occupations × month, 2021.01–2024.12 (~120,000 obs, online job postings) Design Continuous-treatment DID (ChatGPT, Dec 2022) · mediation · quantile regression Exposure GPT-4 occupation-level AI exposure index (PKU × Zhaopin)

Abstract — The public release of ChatGPT in December 2022 marked the transition of generative AI from a niche technology to a widely deployable general-purpose tool. Exploiting this quasi-natural experiment, we construct a city × occupation × month panel from January 2021 to December 2024 using job postings from China's major online recruitment platforms, and employ a continuous-treatment difference-in-differences framework to identify the causal effects of generative AI exposure on skill diversity and wage inequality. We find: (1) AI exposure significantly reduces within-occupation skill diversity (β₂ = −0.054, p < 0.001) but increases between-occupation skill diversity (β₂ = 0.172, p < 0.001) after ChatGPT's release, revealing a "convergence-within, divergence-between" dual pattern; (2) the wage Gini coefficient declines with AI exposure (β₂ = −0.041, p = 0.017), but quantile regression reveals this equalizing effect concentrates at lower quantiles while vanishing at higher quantiles, confirming a "selective equalization" hypothesis; (3) mediation analysis shows that general auxiliary skill displacement mediates within-occupation diversity decline, while occupation-specific auxiliary skill expansion mediates between-occupation diversity divergence; (4) spatial heterogeneity indicates effects are most pronounced in high-GDP cities and medium-exposure industries.

Proposed PhD Research

Generative AI, Skill Diversity, and Wage Inequality: A Computational Sociological Study of China's Urban Labor Markets. The PhD proposal scales the working-paper agenda into sociology: exploiting ChatGPT's 2022 launch as a quasi-natural experiment to investigate how generative AI restructures skill composition and wage inequality across 170 Chinese cities, with PPMI-based skill co-occurrence measures of within- and between-occupation diversity, integrating economic task models (Autor et al., 2003) with sociological theories of occupational closure (Weeden & Grusky, 2005; Abbott, 1988) and the labor process (Braverman, 1974). Empirical strategy: continuous-treatment DID, mediation analysis, and quantile regression on a city × occupation × month panel (~120,000 observations; Python pipeline complete).
